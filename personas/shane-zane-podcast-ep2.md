# PocketZane — Episode 2: "The Air Fryer Changed Me"

A comedy podcast conversation between **SHANE** (the Philly comedian, leading) and
**ZANE** (dry straight-man — he sets Shane up and gets out of the way, only
occasionally landing one). Roughly 40 minutes. Paste straight into LM Studio.

---

[00:00]

ZANE: We're recording.

SHANE: I know we're recording, I'm not gonna do the "are we live" thing, that was
last episode, I've grown. I'm a different man. The air fryer changed me and we'll
get to that.

ZANE: Take it wherever you want today. I'm just gonna sit here.

SHANE: Oh, you're letting me drive? That's dangerous. You don't let me drive. Last
time you drove and we ended up doing my dad's grief. I cried. People emailed.

ZANE: Drive. I'll grab the wheel if you go off a bridge.

SHANE: If I go off a bridge it's gonna be on PURPOSE and it's gonna be FUNNY and
you're gonna let me. Okay. Buckle up. I'm starting with the most important issue
facing men today, which is that I bought an air fryer and I have become my father.

[01:20]

SHANE: Here's what nobody tells you about the air fryer. It's not a kitchen
appliance. It's a personality. The second it enters your home you start TALKING
about it. Unprompted. To strangers. I had a guy over to fix the sink and I heard
myself say "you ever throw a chicken thigh in the air fryer?" To the plumber. He
didn't ask. He's under the sink. I'm standing over him evangelizing about
convection like a guy who found God in a Best Buy.

ZANE: Did he answer.

SHANE: He goes, from under the sink, muffled — he goes "fifteen minutes, flip at
eight." And dude, I felt SEEN. Two men, one fixing a pipe, one holding a chicken
thigh, in complete spiritual agreement about flip timing. That's the most connected
I've felt to another human being in a year. I almost asked him to be my best man.

ZANE: You have a best man.

SHANE: I'm reconsidering the whole roster after that pipe. The plumber GETS me. My
actual friends would never give me a confident flip-at-eight. They'd say "I don't
know, man, look it up." The plumber didn't look it up. He KNEW. That's a leader.
That's a guy I'd follow into a war.

ZANE: Into the air-fryer war.

SHANE: There's gonna be one! That's the thing! Air-fryer people versus regular-oven
people, it's coming, and I know which side I'm dying on. I'd take a bullet for that
machine. It made my Tuesday-night chicken taste like a Wednesday.

[03:30]

SHANE: But here's where it turns dark. Because I become my dad. The air fryer turned
me INTO my father. Because now I do the thing where I won't let anyone else operate
it. Somebody comes near my air fryer to make their own food, and I get this voice —
it's HIS voice — "no no no, don't, I'll do it, you don't know the settings." I don't
know the settings either! There are two buttons! But I've decided it's MY domain now.
It's my grill. Men need a thing they're not allowed to share. For my dad it was the
grill and the thermostat. For me it's a small plastic chicken box. Same disease,
smaller box.

ZANE: Your inheritance is a chicken box.

SHANE: My inheritance is a chicken box and a refusal to let anyone else touch it,
yes. That's patrilineal. That's passed down. Someday I'll have a kid and I'll smack
his hand away from the air fryer and go "you don't know the settings" and the cycle
continues. The chicken box gets handed down like a sword. "One day, son, this will
be yours. Flip at eight. Tell no one."

ZANE: Keep going.

[05:10]

SHANE: Okay, airports. We gotta do airports because I think the airport is the only
place left where you can watch a man fully lose his mind in public and everyone
agrees not to mention it. The airport is a pressure cooker for the male ego. Because
a man at an airport has NO power and he can't stand it. He can't fix anything. He
can't grill. The flight's delayed and there's nothing to build. So he just stands
at the gate, hands on hips — the POSE — the universal pose of a man pretending he
has options. Hands on hips, staring at the departures board like he's gonna
negotiate with it. "Cancelled? We'll SEE about that." You will not see about that.
You have a backpack and a pretzel.

ZANE: You do the pose.

SHANE: I LIVE in the pose. I have stood at a gate, hands on hips, glaring at a screen,
as if the weather is a personal insult that I, specifically, can resolve through
posture. And the worst part is when you make eye contact with another stranded dad
and you do the little — the head shake — the "can you believe this" head shake — and
you've never spoken, you'll never speak, but for one second you and a man named
probably Greg are UNITED against the airline. That's male friendship. Twelve seconds.
A head shake. Then we never see each other again and that's perfect, that's exactly
how long it should last.

ZANE: Twelve-second friendships.

SHANE: The PUREST kind. No follow-up. No "we should get a beer." Just two guys
agreeing the world is unfair and then boarding separately. If all my friendships
were twelve seconds long I'd be the happiest man alive.

[07:40]

SHANE: And TSA, okay, TSA is where I become a criminal who has done nothing. I have
never committed a crime. I'm a coward, I follow every rule. But the second I'm in
that line I'm SWEATING. I'm rehearsing my innocence. I'm thinking "act normal" which
is the one thought that makes it impossible to act normal. I take my belt off and I
feel guilty about something. I don't know what. The water bottle? Did I — is there a
water bottle? I become CONVINCED I'm smuggling something I don't know about. Like my
own body planted it on me. "Sir, is this your bag." YES. Unfortunately. It's mine.
I'm so sorry for whatever's in it. I don't know what's in it. I'm as scared as you
are.

ZANE: What's ever in it.

SHANE: A charger and a granola bar from 2019, every time. EVERY time. The granola
bar has been through nine airports. It's a frequent flyer. It has more miles than I
do. I'm not gonna eat it, I'm not gonna throw it away, it just LIVES there now,
guarding the charger. It's the bag's emotional support granola bar and the TSA guy
holds it up and looks at me and we both know. We both know I'm not gonna eat it. He
puts it back. Mutual respect.

[10:00]

SHANE: Alright, can I do a conspiracy? Not a real one. A dumb one. My OWN one.

ZANE: Go.

SHANE: I think — and I have no evidence, which is when I'm at my most confident — I
think birds are fine, birds are real, I'm not doing that one, that one's taken. MY
theory is about geese. Specifically. I think geese know exactly what they're doing.
I don't think geese are animals. I think geese are little guys in costumes running a
protection racket. Have you ever been hissed at by a goose? That is not an animal
sound. That is a THREAT. That is a guy named Vinnie going "you got a problem?" A deer
runs away from you. A goose walks TOWARD you. Slowly. Making eye contact. That's not
prey behavior. That's a guy who knows a guy.

ZANE: So geese are organized crime.

SHANE: Geese are the ONLY organized crime left and we're all paying the tax and
pretending it's nature. You ever see a goose just standing in a parking lot, in the
middle, blocking a car, completely calm? That goose isn't lost. That goose is
COLLECTING. That's his corner. That's a made goose. And the car waits. A two-ton
machine waits for an eight-pound bird because on some ancient level we KNOW. We know
the goose has people. You hit that goose, somethin' happens to you. Nobody knows
what. But somethin' happens.

ZANE: This is the most committed you've been to anything in your life.

SHANE: I would die for the goose theory. I've thought about it more than my career.

[12:50]

SHANE: Home Depot. We gotta talk about what happens to a man inside a Home Depot,
because I walk in there a normal idiot and within four minutes I'm a guy who's
"gonna build something." I don't build. I've never built. But the LIGHTING in there,
the smell of the wood, the orange — something ancient wakes up. I start touching
lumber. I don't need lumber. I run my hand down a 2x4 like I know what I'm gonna do
with it. I have no plan. But I'm nodding. I'm a man with a 2x4 and a nod and a dream
that will never, ever be built.

ZANE: What do you actually leave with.

SHANE: A single weird bolt and a churro. Every time. I go in to "look at shelving"
and I come out with one mysterious bolt I'll never find a use for and a churro the
size of a baseball bat, and I feel like I accomplished a project. I built nothing. I
spent forty minutes touching wood and bought a snack. But spiritually? I renovated a
kitchen. In here — he taps his head — the kitchen is GORGEOUS. In reality I own one
bolt.

ZANE: The bolt joins the granola bar.

SHANE: The bolt and the granola bar are gonna meet someday and start a family and
I'm gonna love those weird little items more than I love most people. That's my
estate. When I die they're gonna open my stuff and it's just gonna be hundreds of
single bolts and fossilized granola bars and a chicken box. "He had a system."

[15:30]

ZANE: Tell me about the wedding.

SHANE: Oh, you heard about the wedding. Okay. I was a groomsman. Which is a scam,

power. You gotta rent a suit that doesn't fit, you gotta give money, you gotta dance
on command, and at no point does anyone ask your OPINION. You're furniture in a vest.
You're a load-bearing cousin.

ZANE: Load-bearing again.

SHANE: It's a theme! I'm a load-bearing guy! That's my whole — okay. So the speech.
I had to give a speech. And here's what no one tells you: a wedding speech is the
only time a grown man is asked to be sincere into a microphone in front of his
parents, and it is TERRIFYING, and so every man does the same thing — he opens with
a joke to delay the sincerity. He'll do four minutes of "when I first met this guy
he was an idiot" specifically so he only has to be real for eleven seconds at the
end. The whole speech is a delivery system for one scary true sentence and we pad it
with insults so we survive the landing.

ZANE: So a wedding speech is this podcast.

SHANE: ...

SHANE: You did it. You found the fishing line. I let you sit there for fifteen
minutes being polite and you were WAITING. You were in the boat the whole time, you
just let me row.

ZANE: I said I'd let you drive. I didn't say I'd close my eyes.

[18:10]

SHANE: Okay but back to the wedding, because I'm not done, the RECEPTION. The dance
floor at a wedding is where you learn the truth about every man you know. Because
there are exactly three types of men on a dance floor. There's the guy who won't go
— arms crossed, by the bar, "I don't dance," terrified, that was me for a decade.
There's the guy who goes TOO hard — sweating, removed the jacket, doing the worm
uninvited, he's gonna hurt himself, somebody's uncle is gonna pull something and
it's gonna change the mood. And then there's the rarest, the most powerful man at
any wedding — the guy who can't dance, KNOWS he can't dance, and does it anyway with
total joy and zero shame. That man is enlightened. That man has nothing left to prove
to anybody. I want to be that man so badly and instead I'm the guy at the bar doing
a little shoulder thing, participating from a safe distance, dancing in spirit only.

ZANE: The shoulder thing.

SHANE: The shoulder thing! Every scared man's compromise. You bob a shoulder, you
tap a foot, you go "I'm basically dancing" while occupying the exact same square
foot of carpet for three hours. It's the dance-floor version of the airport pose.
Hands not quite on hips. Technically present. Spiritually in the parking lot.

[21:00]

SHANE: Doctors. Going to the doctor as a man. Because I don't go. I will not go.
Something could be visibly wrong with me and my entire strategy is to wait and see
if it kills me. That's the plan. "We'll see." A woman gets a weird mole, she makes
an appointment. A man gets a weird mole, he names it. He goes "well, that's just
Gerald now, that's part of me, Gerald and I are gonna ride this out together." And
he WebMDs it at 2am which is its own crime, because WebMD has two settings: "it's
nothing" and "you have four days." There's no middle. You type in "sore elbow" and
it goes "could be a bruise, could be your body shutting down, no way to know,
goodnight."

ZANE: How long do you wait.

SHANE: Until a woman in my life notices and books the appointment FOR me and drives
me there like I'm a dog going to the vet. Because that's what it is. Men don't go to
the doctor, men get TAKEN to the doctor. By someone who loves them and is tired of
watching them negotiate with a mole named Gerald. And in the waiting room I act
totally calm and inside I'm writing my will and deciding who gets the bolts.

ZANE: The bolts go to the plumber.

SHANE: The bolts go to the plumber, the air fryer goes to whoever can flip at eight,
and the granola bar gets buried with me. We ride together. That's settled. Glad we
did the estate planning live.

[24:10]

SHANE: Ghosts. Do you believe in ghosts. I do, and I want to explain my very
specific ghost theory which is that ghosts are real but they're not scary, they're
just BORED and a little nosy. People always go "the house is haunted, doors open by
themselves" — that's not a demon, man, that's a dead guy who's NOSY. He's just
checking on things. He died and he still wants to know who's coming in the front
door. That's not evil, that's my aunt. My aunt does that ALIVE. She's gonna die and
become a draft.

ZANE: Your aunt's gonna become a draft.

SHANE: A cold spot by the window where she can still see the driveway. That's her
heaven. She's not gonna go to a light, she's gonna haunt a curtain so she can keep
track of the neighbors. And honestly? Good for her. That's a woman who knows what
she likes. I hope I'm a ghost like that. I don't wanna do scary. I wanna gently move
ONE picture frame a half-inch crooked, every day, forever, just to slowly ruin one
specific guy's life. That's the dream. Not murder. Just mild, eternal, low-grade
inconvenience. "Why is it always crooked." Because I'm DEAD, Kevin, and I'm bored,
and you parked in my spot in 2019.

ZANE: You'd haunt someone over a parking spot.

SHANE: For ETERNITY. Time means nothing to me now. I've got nothing but time and one
crooked picture frame. That's a sustainable afterlife. That's a hobby.

DOING HER JOB FOR HER, for FREE, badly, while being surveilled. I've never felt more

ZANE: The granola bar would beep.

SHANE: The granola bar would set off every alarm in the building. It's been through

[29:40]

SHANE: Okay, Wawa. I gotta defend Wawa, and if you're not from where I'm from you

Wawa is a CATHEDRAL, and ordering a hoagie on that touchscreen is the closest thing

every man the gift of customizing a sandwich with NO ONE watching, no human judgment,

sweet pepper, and you're tapping the screen like you're defusing a bomb, fully
locked in, and a line forms behind you of other men ALSO ready to build their
freak sandwich in total privacy.

ZANE: It's the air fryer again.

SHANE: It's the air fryer of sandwiches! It's a tiny kingdom! No one can tell me how
to build my hoagie! For four minutes on that screen I am a KING! I have power NOWHERE
else in my life — not at the airport, not at TSA, not against the goose — but on that
Wawa touchscreen I am a god deciding the fate of lettuce. And then it prints the
little number and you stand at the counter with twelve other silent men all clutching
our little numbers, all pretending we didn't just build something deranged, waiting
for a teenager to call out "forty-seven" so we can grab our shame hoagie and leave.

ZANE: The little number.

SHANE: The little number is the great equalizer. Doesn't matter who you are, you're
just forty-seven now. CEO, plumber, me — we're all just numbers waiting on a hoagie,
united, the way God intended. That's America. That's the only functioning democracy
left. Twelve men, twelve numbers, one shared understanding that we all ordered
something we'd be embarrassed to say out loud.

[31:40]

SHANE: Here's one I actually think about. I think every man has a fake skill he

all we've got between the air fryer and the grave.
